How to Sell an Inherited House Fast (Without the Stress)

Published July 22, 2026 | By Melo Haven Real Estate Solutions

Inheriting a property sounds like a windfall until you realize what comes with it: property taxes, insurance, maintenance, potential mortgage payments, and the emotional weight of dealing with a loved one's estate. If you've inherited a house you don't want — or can't afford to keep — here's how to sell it fast.

Do You Need to Go Through Probate First?

It depends on your state and how the property was titled:

  • If there's a will naming you as beneficiary — the property still typically goes through probate, but it's usually straightforward.
  • If the property was in a living trust — you can likely skip probate entirely and sell immediately.
  • If there's no will (intestate) — the property goes through probate and the court determines who inherits. This takes longer but doesn't prevent you from selling once it's resolved.
  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship — ownership transfers automatically to the surviving owner. No probate needed.

The key takeaway: even if probate is required, you can often begin the sales process during probate with court approval. An experienced buyer like Melo Haven can work with properties in probate.

The Hidden Costs of Keeping an Inherited House

Many heirs don't realize how expensive it is to hold a property they're not living in:

  • Property taxes — due whether you live there or not. Delinquent taxes accrue penalties and interest.
  • Insurance — vacant properties require special (more expensive) insurance policies.
  • Maintenance — lawns, plumbing, HVAC, roof leaks don't stop just because no one lives there.
  • Utilities — you typically need to keep water and electricity on to prevent pipe bursts and maintain the property.
  • Mortgage payments — if there's an existing mortgage, payments are due immediately. Missing them starts the foreclosure clock.
  • Liability — you're responsible if someone gets injured on the property.

Every month you hold a vacant inherited property, you're losing money. The sooner you sell, the more equity you preserve.

Your 3 Options for Selling an Inherited House

Option 1: Sell to a Cash Home Buyer (Fastest)

A cash buyer like Melo Haven can purchase the property as-is — no repairs, no cleaning out, no staging. We can close in as little as 7-14 days. This is the best option when:

Option 2: List with a Real Estate Agent

If the property is in good condition and you're not in a rush, listing with an agent can maximize your sale price. Expect 30-90 days on market, 5-6% in commissions, plus repair and staging costs. You'll also need to clean out the property and handle all showings.

Option 3: Rent It Out

If the property is in rentable condition and you want ongoing income, becoming a landlord is an option. But it comes with tenant management, maintenance costs, and the responsibilities of being a property owner — which may be exactly what you're trying to avoid.

What About Capital Gains Tax on Inherited Property?

Good news: inherited property receives a "stepped-up basis", meaning your cost basis is the fair market value at the time of the decedent's death — not what they originally paid. If your parent bought a house for $100,000 in 1990 and it's worth $350,000 when they pass, your basis is $350,000. If you sell for $350,000, you owe zero capital gains tax.

This is a significant tax advantage, but it only works in your favor if you sell relatively soon after inheriting. The longer you hold, the more potential capital gains accumulate above the stepped-up basis.

Selling an Inherited House with Multiple Heirs

When multiple family members inherit a property, disagreements are common. Some want to sell, others want to keep it. Here's the reality: if even one heir wants to sell, they can petition the court for a partition sale. It's better for everyone to agree on a sale and split the proceeds than to go through a costly legal battle.

Cash buyers make this easier — a fast, clean sale with proceeds split equally among heirs. No months of negotiation, no repair disputes, no realtor showing the property while family members argue.
